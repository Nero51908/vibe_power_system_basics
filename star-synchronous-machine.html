Power systems / worked example / 50 Hz

The star-connected synchronous machine

The same 500 kVA, 12-lead alternator as the delta page, with its links moved back to series star: 415 V between lines, 240 V from each line to the neutral, four wires out. Nothing inside the machine has changed, and at the same 400 kW, 0.85 lagging each winding sees exactly what it saw in delta: 240 V, 653.6 A, the same EMF, the same load angle. What changes is which terminal number equals which winding number. In star the line current is the winding current and the line voltage is not the winding voltage; the √3 and the 30° have moved from the currents to the voltages. Everything is live; drag the deck.

Step 1: the star, alive

Three windings from one point, four wires out

Here is the machine as the fitter wired it this time: the three windings meet at the star point n, a line conductor leaves each far end, and a fourth conductor, the neutral, leaves n. Run the film and watch the currents themselves. Two things hold at every frame. The current in winding an is the current in line a: one path, one measurement, two names. The voltage across winding an is not the voltage between lines a and b; that one spans two windings. And the neutral carries the superposition of the three line currents, which with balance is exactly nothing.

Step 2: two windings, one voltage, the first 30°

Between two terminals

The voltage between lines a and b spans two windings: from a down winding an to the star point, then up winding bn to b. It is the superposition of the two winding voltages, one of them taken the other way round: V_ab = V_an − V_bn (the same sum written as V_an + V_nb is one button away). On the left the arrows close that superposition as a triangle; on the right it happens height by height at the time cursor. Two equal arrows 120° apart combine into an arrow √3 as long, sitting 30° ahead of the first; that is where the line-to-winding √3 and the 30° both come from, on the voltage side this time. Flip the sequence in the deck and the 30° changes side.

Step 3: the second 30°, and why they cancel

Two shifts, one angle

Step 2 gave the voltage its 30°. The current has one too, but only in the equivalent delta: the delta-equivalent winding current I_ab, what the same line current would be inside a delta-connected twin of this machine, is I_a/√3 and sits 30° ahead of I_a. Line voltage leads winding voltage by 30°; delta-equivalent winding current leads line current by 30°; so the angle from V_ab to I_ab is the same φ as the angle from V_an to I_a. Pair the line-to-line voltage with the line current instead and you read φ + 30°, the oscilloscope trap. And because every arrow turns at the same ω, all four keep their relative angles in every frame: that rigid rotation is the whole licence for reading a running machine off one photograph.

Step 4: inside one winding (E, V and the quarter turn)

Where δ lives

Inside winding an the field produces an EMF E_an; what reaches the terminals is V_an. The difference is the drop across the synchronous reactance, and X_s is not a phasor. It has no wave. It is an instruction: take I_a, turn it a quarter turn ahead, scale it by X_s. The result of that instruction is a phasor, j·X_s·I_a, and it has a wave; lay it on the tip of V_an and E_an closes the triangle. This triangle is the delta page's triangle to the last volt: the winding does not know how it is linked. The angle δ between E and V is the load angle, and on the right it is a time: e_an(t) crosses zero before v_an(t) by δ/360°·T. More torque moves δ, not the speed; both waves keep the same 20 ms.

Step 5: break the balance

What the neutral is for

Three independent load sliders: the neutral current appears as the superposition of three unequal line currents, the three terminal voltages drift apart through their own X_s drops, and V_LL = √3·V_LN stops being true. With the honest chip that a single X_s per winding is a first-order picture of unbalanced operation.

Step 6: diagnostics

Four ways to misread the star

Each misreading leaves a fingerprint. The cards recompute from the deck; if your number disagrees with the page by one of these exact patterns, you have found the mistake.

Line-to-line voltage in the per-phase equation

E = V_ab + j·X_s·I_a  (should be V_an) –×√3 voltage, twisted 30°

–. The winding sees its own voltage. Plugging the line-to-line voltage in starts the triangle √3 too far out and 30° early; E comes out far too large and δ far too small.

The 30° trap

pf = cos ∠(V_ab, I_a) = cos(φ ± 30°) –30° too much (or too little) lag

–. A scope across a-b with a clamp on line a reads the angle between a line-to-line voltage and a line current. Element voltage against element current, always (Step 3).

Delta equivalent with the star X_s

E_ab = V_ab + j·X_s·I_ab  (should be 3·X_s) –÷3 reactance

–. Three windings of X_s in star look like three of 3·X_s in delta (Z_Δ = 3·Z_Y). Carry the star ohms into the delta picture and the triangle is drawn three times too flat.

"A lagging generator absorbs vars"

Q = 3·V_an·(E_an·cos δ − V_an)/X_s –sign trap

In generator convention a lagging current means the machine is over-excited and supplies vars. "Absorbs" is what the same two words mean under motor convention. The var direction is physics; lag and lead are bookkeeping, which is why this page prints the direction beside every lag/lead label.
