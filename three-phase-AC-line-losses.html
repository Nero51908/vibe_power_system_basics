Step 1 · One phase tells the whole story

The per-phase circuit — with the wire we don't build

Balance lets you collapse three phases into one single-phase circuit between a line and the neutral reference. Its source is V_LN = V_LL/√3, it carries one third of the power, and its return rail is the neutral — which carries no current, which is exactly why the real line has three wires, not four.

Sanity check the current here: I = (P/3) / (V_LN · cos φ). If you pasted V_LL into this circuit instead, you'd get a current √3 too small — and losses exactly 3× too small. That factor of 3 is a diagnostic fingerprint.

Step 3 · Where you stand changes what you read

Walk the line — power factor is a property of a cross-section

On a series line the current phasor is the same everywhere, so as you walk from the load toward the source all the movement is in V(x) = V_R + I·(r + j·x_L)·x. The resistive drop stretches V along I; the reactive drop is perpendicular and rotates it. The local angle φ(x) between V(x) and I — and with it the local power factor — drifts with position: your unity-pf load, read from 120 km away, is not unity any more.

Step 5 · Substitute back — the identity that explains the grid

P_loss = R·P² / (V_LL·cos φ)² — the loss cliff

Eliminate the current and the whole history of transmission falls out of one line: for fixed delivered power, losses scale as 1/V² and 1/pf². Double the voltage, quarter the loss. Flip to log–log and the law becomes a straight line of slope −2 — power laws always do.

The chart is a control: drag anywhere on the plot to slide the operating voltage, or tap a dot to snap to a standard level — the whole page follows. Dots mark standard Australian voltage levels for the same conductor and load — left of the wall the model (and the conductor) melts; the trend is the point. In reality higher-voltage lines also carry heavier bundles, so real losses fall even faster than 1/V².
Step 6 · Diagnostics

Three ways to be wrong by exactly 3 (or √3)

These aren't random errors — each has a fingerprint. If two students' answers differ by exactly 3 or √3, hunt the V_LL/V_LN swap or the conductor count. The cards recompute from the sliders.

The single-phase paste

I = P / (V_LL · cos φ) = – A
P_loss = 3·I²·R = – –×3 too high

Treated 275 kV as if it sat across each element carrying the full power. The missing √3 in the current becomes a clean factor of 3 in the loss.

The lonely conductor

P_loss = I²·R (correct I, no 3) –×3 too low

Computed one conductor of three. Same fingerprint as pasting V_LL into the per-phase circuit — which is why the factor alone doesn't tell you which mistake was made.

The geometry impostor

P_loss = √3·I²·R –×√3 too low

The triangle's √3 crept into the conductor count's seat. √3 relates two voltage measurements; 3 counts wires. Say both out loud when you write the formula.

Coda · Why the ladder exists

Every rung is a factor of 1/V²

The identity above is not just an exam formula — it is the selection pressure that built the grid. Each generation of engineers climbed one rung of voltage to push the same power with a quarter of the burn (and each rung demanded earthed-wye insulation economics).
